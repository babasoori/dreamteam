01 · The game

Build software by running a basketball team.

Dreamteam is a local agent orchestration tool for one project. You are the coach and set the objective. A point guard organizes the work. The other positions contribute implementation, integration, testing, and independent review. A sixth man provides relief or a missing specialty.

The basketball model is the product’s operating language: roster means the available players, starting five means PG, SG, SF, PF, and C, possession means one bounded task, and having the ball means being accountable for its next action. A pass transfers that responsibility with the evidence needed to continue.

First success: open the Courtside TUI, call one small play, see who has the ball, produce a real code change, test and review the same candidate, and accept the result from the TUI. Every position has a defined job; players run when the play needs them. A polished, usable TUI is part of the demo’s acceptance criteria.

You are the coach. You call and run the plays, direct passes, make substitutions, and accept the results. The roster contains six Codex players: the starting five and The Answer on the bench. Coaching stays with you.

Hackathon context: use the day to finish a working possession and demonstrate it. The event is context; this design introduces no sponsor-tool requirement. Event provided for context.

Air Jordan · Michael Jordan · SG

Basketball: elite scoring paired with elite defense. NBA profile.

Agent persona: the focused finisher. Own the primary implementation, complete the requested behavior, and check the patch before passing.

King James · LeBron James · SF

Basketball: combines scoring with exceptional passing. NBA feature.

Agent persona: the versatile connector. Follow the complete user flow, bridge components, and check that interfaces agree.

Sir Charles · Charles Barkley · PF

Basketball: an undersized power forward whose persistence and rebounding let him compete with much taller opponents. Hall of Fame profile.

Agent persona: the persistent tester. Chase missed cases, reproduce failures, and make every defect actionable.

Shaq · Shaquille O’Neal · C

Basketball: a physically imposing scorer, twice the league’s scoring champion and three times Finals MVP. Hall of Fame profile.

Agent persona: the team’s anchor for independent review. Challenge fragile assumptions and require concrete evidence on critical behavior.

Signature move — hold the paint: review the exact candidate and return specific findings or a supported acceptance recommendation. This review assignment is our analogy to his presence and dominance.

The Answer · Allen Iverson · 6th Man

Basketball: an explosive scorer whose quickness and ball handling helped him create opportunities despite his size; a four-time scoring champion. Hall of Fame profile.

Agent persona: the inventive problem solver off your bench. When you call him in, take the handoff, investigate the blocker, and find a focused way forward within the inherited assignment.

Signature move — the crossover: approach a stuck problem from a different angle, test the idea, and return a concrete result. Sixth man is his role on your Dream Team.

Your roster: five starters plus The Answer on the bench. You decide when The Answer enters the play and whose assignment he takes over. If The Answer contributes code, another player provides the independent review.

Player context researched 11 October 2026 using NBA and Naismith Basketball Hall of Fame sources. Names, role briefs, and signature moves are enough for the first demo; a points or badges system can wait.

The sixth man replaces a player. A substitution benches the outgoing player for that assignment and records who now occupies the position. The relief player receives the brief, current candidate, and latest handoff in its own Codex session; conversation history remains with the original player session.

03 · The first play

Run one possession from brief to basket.

Choose a small change in a familiar repository with a quick, observable check. A suitable example is adding a filter to an existing list view and verifying its empty state. Keep the possession narrow enough to explain and demonstrate in a few minutes.

  1. You call the play. Use New Play to write the goal, constraints, and acceptance criteria. Give the ball to Penny through the confirmed-pass procedure below.
  2. Penny (PG) sets up the offense. Inspect the relevant code and write a short plan. Identify any SF integration work and the checks PF should run.
  3. Air Jordan (SG) takes the shot. Implement the main change and record the candidate and checks already performed.
  4. King James (SF) connects the play. Check the interfaces and user flow. If integration work is needed, give SF sole edit ownership before passing on. Otherwise record the check.
  5. Sir Charles (PF) tests the possession. Run the focused checks against the resulting candidate and document failures or missing coverage.
  6. Shaq (C) checks the candidate. Review that same candidate and PF’s evidence. Return specific findings or a recommendation to accept.
  7. You record the score. Accept only when the criteria are satisfied. Otherwise send a revision through PG. Changed candidates need fresh relevant checks and review.

A position may sit out a play when it has no useful contribution; the coach records that choice. Defining the starting five does not require five simultaneous processes or five ritual responses to every task.

Every pass carries context

  • Possession ID, goal, and acceptance criteria.
  • Outgoing player, receiving player, and requested next action.
  • Exact candidate: repository and revision or saved patch.
  • Completed checks, findings, and blockers.

One explicit transfer

The outgoing player writes the handoff. From Courtside, you attach to the receiving player’s native terminal, deliver the handoff, and confirm receipt. Return to the TUI and use Pass to record the transfer. Then reattach and tell the receiving player to begin. The receiver waits for that explicit go-ahead. Use the same procedure for Penny’s initial assignment; the TUI does not send a start-work prompt. If delivery is uncertain, inspect the terminal before continuing.

The prior owner remains accountable until the coach confirms transfer. Writing a note or observing an idle terminal never moves the ball automatically.

Bring in the sixth man when useful: if a player is blocked or lacks a needed specialty, call a substitution, preserve the possession, and continue from the handoff. Demonstrate this after the basic possession works.

What stays visible

  • Coach sidebar entry: selects an interactive Codex conversation that reads team state and sends bounded assignments when you ask. It is separate from every player terminal.
  • Roster rail and bench: each name, position, and session activity. Selection and ball ownership have distinct markers.
  • Main workspace: Coach's Codex conversation or the selected player's terminal, with a compact saved-play summary. Future handoffs, checks, reviews, and blockers belong with the recorded play evidence.
  • Pass history: recorded transfers and meaningful task events, including failures and acceptance.
  • Keyboard footer: visible controls and a clear focus state.

Visual direction

Use the visual language of coding-agent CLIs: a compact identity and project-path header, near-black background, cool gray text, and muted metadata. Borrow Herdr’s slim session navigation and Fresh’s compact, text-led terminal conventions. A single divider separates the roster from the main workspace; avoid dashboard cards, heavy frames, and large button treatments.

Keep basketball in the names, positions, starting five, bench, possessions, and passes. Blend Ghostty’s Ultra Dark with Nord and Tokyo Night: use near-black #080a0f for the base, Nord’s #d8dee9 for text, Tokyo Night’s #7aa2f7 for selection and key hints, and #bb9af7 for occasional violet accents. Keep green for idle, amber for blocked, and rose for errors. Pair activity markers with readable words: working, idle, blocked, stopped, or unknown. Render coaching actions as plain text hints. A focused player terminal accepts real Codex input; Ctrl+G returns to coaching. Natural-language team requests go to Coach's own Codex conversation; direct player input goes only to the selected player.

At 120 columns, leave generous room for the main workspace. At 80 columns, tighten spacing while keeping Coach and all six names and activity labels visible. The project path, selection, input-focus guidance, and keyboard controls remain readable.

05 · Under the hood

Keep the implementation small enough to finish.

Keep Python 3.12+ and add Textual for the required TUI. Use the standard library for configuration, subprocess calls, and local state. A few ordinary Python functions implement player lifecycle, possession, pass, and acceptance actions; the TUI calls them directly. One concrete Herdr wrapper handles bounded control calls and terminal attachment. No model SDK is needed while Codex owns the agent loops.

Use Textual containers and widgets for the roster rail, main workspace, forms, and event feed. Run bounded Herdr queries in background workers so refreshing activity cannot freeze keyboard input. Avoid overlapping refreshes; apply results on the UI thread. When a query fails, preserve the last observation with its age and mark current activity unknown.

For inline control, verify the selected player's runtime binding and open Herdr's NDJSON terminal controller against its immutable terminal ID. Render bounded ANSI frames with pyte; forward input and resize messages only after the controller grants the session. Keep the sidebar's Coach entry and roster visible while the main workspace shows the selected player's terminal. Ctrl+G, changing players, or leaving Courtside releases and reaps the client. No new pane or agent is created by inline control. Poll the selected player's read-only viewport while detached; label failed reads stale. One interactive attachment owns input at a time, with no automatic takeover.

O retains the optional Open alongside action, which creates or reuses an owned sibling viewer. Outside Herdr or when a split will not fit, show the explicit dreamteam --view PLAYER command. Quit offers Stop team and quit by default, with Keep running as an explicit choice. Verified cleanup closes owned viewer/player panes and empty named servers, preserves active user terminals, and reports remaining resources. A detached or failed native viewer exits rather than waiting in a reconnect loop.

Interactive Coach console: you direct a separate Codex conversation from the Coach sidebar entry. Per-session stdio tools expose team status, recent player output, bounded dispatch, progress reports, short waits, and creation of the one saved play. No model SDK, API key, network listener, or global Codex configuration change is needed. A dispatch records intent before delivery, verifies the named session, and does not blindly retry uncertain input. New player sessions report through an actor-bound tool; Coach can record an explicit visible result from an older session with separate provenance. Status and reports are checked on request, not by a perpetual background watcher. Your acceptance remains separate from reported completion.

Coach shares the same terminal lifecycle as the players. Stopping the team stops Coach first. A tool process is pinned to that Coach launch and cannot dispatch after it is stopped or replaced. Changing sidebar views releases only the input client; it keeps the selected conversation available for later.

Implementation references: Textual layouts, background workers, Herdr terminal sessions, and pyte screen parsing.

06 · Rules of play

A basket needs evidence.

  • One ball, one accountable owner. Keep a single writer for the active candidate. Parallel edits and automatic worktree management wait until a later version.
  • Freeze the candidate for checks and review. PF and C refer to the same identifiable revision or saved patch. New edits invalidate the relevant earlier evidence.
  • Activity is not the score. Working, idle, blocked, stopped, and unknown describe sessions. Planned, in progress, in review, and accepted describe the possession. Only the coach accepts.
  • Unknown stays unknown. An unreachable backend does not prove a player stopped. Verify session, pane, and project identity before start, attach, or stop. Refuse ambiguous targets.
  • Repeated start does not duplicate a player. Reuse a verified live binding. Stop only the selected Dreamteam-owned pane; never the user’s entire Herdr server.
  • A substitution preserves the play. Record the outgoing player, incoming player, inherited position, candidate, and handoff. Do not transfer responsibility to two players at once.
  • Native approvals stay visible. Let Codex ask for approvals in each player session. Detaching from a terminal and recovering after a server restart are separate behaviors to verify.
  • Automation comes with an explicit completion signal. If programmatic delivery is added, inspect after a timeout before retrying. Do not equate idle, successful input delivery, or a completed wait with a finished assignment.
07 · Today’s build

Build Courtside, then run one complete possession.

The target is a polished, responsive TUI driving a live, repeatable possession: a real diff, visible passes, check evidence, and your acceptance. Build one screen and its essential actions first. Reassess the time available after the Herdr integration check; the TUI remains part of the demo when scope is reduced.

1. Prove the terminal handoff

Use a minimal Textual screen and one isolated Herdr session. Create the pane, launch Codex, attach from the TUI, interact with approvals, detach, and return to the screen. Reconnect and stop only that owned pane. Inspect actual identifiers and response shapes, then verify a second named Codex player has a separate conversation and runtime binding.

2. Build the Courtside screen

Extend the terminal roster and bench with possession details, the selected player’s handoff, an event feed, and plain keyboard hints. Add roster validation and wire up start, attach, refresh, and stop through the same Python actions. Give each player its role brief. Verify duplicate-start protection and stale or ambiguous bindings. Preserve the near-black terminal palette, sparse text layout, cool Nord text, and restrained Tokyo Night accents.

3. Wire the coaching controls

New Play is implemented. Next add confirmed Pass and Accept forms, candidate evidence, and handoff history to the saved possession. Keep assignment delivery in native terminals. Verify selection, form validation, incomplete-handoff rejection, and acceptance against the current candidate. Idle never scores a possession. Use focused Textual interaction checks plus a live Herdr smoke check.

4. Polish and rehearse the live demo

Complete one small change from Courtside, collect PF’s checks and C’s review, and record your decision in the TUI. Verify attach-and-return, keyboard focus, resize behavior, readable contrast, and an unavailable-backend state. Rehearse with the actual terminal and capture the real run. Try a sixth-man substitution only after the main flow works.

If time gets tight

Keep the TUI, all six player cards, player selection, native attachment, current possession, pass history, and explicit acceptance. Run a shorter PG → SG → C possession inside Courtside. You run and record the checks and mark unused positions as sitting out.

Cut animations, elaborate court art, searchable history, standalone CLI command parity, and substitution controls before reducing the core screen. Preserve clear typography, spacing, status labels, and a real change with review and acceptance.

After a working demo

Add The Answer’s substitutions if they did not make today’s cut. Refine the plays and handoff shortcuts based on what helps you coach the team.

Concurrent possessions, automated worktrees, remote operation, budgets, and automatic merging stay outside today’s scope.
